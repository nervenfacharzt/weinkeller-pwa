Zweigelt: Austria's most-drunk red, and why it rarely wants waiting

The commonest mistake with this variety is not poor storage but storage that lasts too long.

Last updated: 2026-07-26

At a glance

Origin
Austria, bred in 1922 at Klosterneuburg
Key regions
Lower Austria, Burgenland, Styria
Character
Velvety, juicy, soft tannin, dark cherry
Ageing potential
1 to 10 years, usually the lower end

Zweigelt is Austria's most widely planted red variety and sits in more Austrian cellars than any other red grape. It is also mishandled more often than most — not on temperature, but on time. The sentence this page turns on: the great majority of Zweigelt bottles are made to be drunk soon, and keeping them costs you the thing you bought them for.

Origin and parentage

The variety was bred in 1922 at the federal college and research institute in Klosterneuburg, as a cross of St Laurent and Blaufränkisch. It was first called Rotburger and has only carried its breeder's name since the 1970s; both terms appear on labels, as does “Blauer Zweigelt”.

The parentage accounts for almost the whole character. From St Laurent come the velvet texture and the dark cherry fruit; from Blaufränkisch the backbone that shows in the better bottlings. In practice the softer parent usually wins. Zweigelt also ripens earlier and more reliably than Blaufränkisch and yields usable crops in awkward years — which is what made it so widespread, and also why a great deal of undistinguished wine goes out under the name. At high yields it is prone to bunch stem necrosis, so the better wines nearly always come from reduced crops.

What is in the glass

Sour cherry and dark cherry are typical, often a note of plum, sometimes a touch of violet. The colour runs from mid-dark to dark, but the palate is considerably softer than the appearance suggests: little perceptible tannin, moderate acidity, a juicy flow. That is not a weakness; it is the variety's actual quality. A good Zweigelt tastes effortless and does not ask to be admired.

The same profile explains the short life. Tannin and acidity are the two substances that carry a red wine through years. Where both are only moderate, there is little for a wine to hold onto. The fruit fades, and what remains is a thin, faintly bitter residue — not faulty, but empty.

The exceptions: Reserve and single-vineyard wines

There is Zweigelt that holds for several years, and it comes from producers who deliberately worked against the natural profile: low yields, late picking, longer maceration, ageing in small oak. Such wines carry more extract and tannin, and over five to ten years develop tertiary notes of tobacco, leather and dried cherry. You can spot them on the label, through three signals that usually appear together.

  • A vineyard or Reserve designation. “Ried” plus a vineyard name, the word “Reserve”, or a region's top category — this is the strongest indicator. In Carnuntum and around Lake Neusiedl, Zweigelt carries exactly these upper tiers.
  • A price clearly above the same estate's entry bottle. Cask ageing costs money and time, and that shows up in the price. As a rule of thumb: an estate's cheapest Zweigelt is meant for drinking, its dearest for waiting.
  • A later bottling or release date. When a producer sells the simple Zweigelt in the spring after harvest but the single-vineyard wine two years later, that is a statement of intent.

If all three signals are absent, assume a wine for drinking. That is not a criticism. Most Austrian Zweigelt is made for exactly that purpose and is hard to beat in the role.

Zweigelt in blends

As a blending component the variety turns up more often than people assume. It contributes colour, fruit and soft texture, so it is often paired with more structural partners: Blaufränkisch, Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah. Such blends usually carry a proprietary name, with the varietal shares in the small print. There the Zweigelt profile does not govern storage — the wine as a whole does, and the more tannic varieties and oak involved, the longer the range. Zweigelt is also a mainstay of Austrian rosé, which belongs in the year after bottling rather than in a shelf slot marked for later.

Rough ranges

The figures below are rules of thumb and should be read as ranges. Vintage, site and the producer's hand shift them in both directions.

Type How to recognise it Usual range from the vintage
Zweigelt rosé Pale colour, variety named, low price roughly 1 to 2 years
Classic, steel or large oak Variety name alone, often a litre bottle, screwcap roughly 1 to 3 years
Regional or village wine Origin named, a little more structure, mid price roughly 2 to 5 years
Reserve or single vineyard, barrique Ried or Reserve on the label, later release, top price roughly 4 to 10 years

A Reserve is still too young when the oak stands beside the fruit as a separate layer, often as vanilla or a toasted note, and the wine feels broader on the palate than the finish can support. A bottle is past it — the commoner case here — when the fruit is dry and dull rather than juicy, the rim shades towards brown, and the wine reads as thin rather than soft. Zweigelt rarely collapses dramatically; it goes quieter until nothing is left.

What to watch when laying it down

The most important point is a decision rather than a storage condition: separate the drinking bottles from the waiting bottles physically when you put them away. Zweigelt is the variety where a mixed shelf costs most, because the simple bottles disappear among the expensive ones and turn up again four years later.

The drinking bottles need no cellar in the strict sense. A cool, dark cupboard handles two years without difficulty, particularly as a large share is bottled under screwcap and so needs neither to lie down nor to worry about humidity. A case bought is consumption, not a reserve.

For Reserve and single-vineyard wines the usual rules apply, since natural cork is almost always involved: store lying down, keep the temperature even, sixty to eighty per cent humidity, no light. And in both cases what holds for any collection holds here too. Without a recorded drinking window, chance decides when a bottle gets opened (how to set that up is covered separately) — and with short windows, that chance is expensive.

Ageing estimates are rules of thumb, not guarantees. How long a given bottle carries depends on the vintage, the winemaking and above all on storage. When in doubt, open one bottle earlier than planned.
