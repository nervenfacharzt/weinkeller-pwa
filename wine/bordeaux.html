Bordeaux: left bank, right bank, and what it means for your cellar

No other name on the shelf covers so much ground: everyday bottles finished at three years, and wines that have barely started at twenty.

Last updated: 2026-07-26

At a glance

Country & region
France, south-west, around the Gironde
Key varieties
Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc
Character
Structured, savoury, almost always a blend
Ageing potential
2 to 30 years depending on tier

Bordeaux describes an origin, not a style, and it is a very large origin. The same name on the shelf covers wines that are at their best after two years and wines that give away nothing at all after two years. Anyone laying down Bordeaux needs to answer one question first: which of those two kinds of bottle is this. Everything else follows from that.

Where the character comes from

The region sits on the Atlantic coast, around the point where the Garonne and the Dordogne meet to form the Gironde estuary. The climate is maritime: mild winters, no great heat, and no guarantees in autumn. Rain during harvest is a normal risk here rather than an exception, and that risk produced the region's defining habit — blending. If several varieties ripen at different moments, a difficult September does not fall on a single point of failure. A single-variety wine would have no such insurance.

Soil supplies the other half of the explanation. Cabernet Sauvignon ripens late, has thick skins and needs warm, free-draining ground; the gravel terraces on the left bank of the Gironde provide exactly that. Merlot ripens earlier and copes with the cooler, heavier clay and limestone soils that dominate the right bank. The famous split between the two banks is therefore not an aesthetic choice. It is a consequence of which grape can reliably ripen where.

Left bank and right bank

The left bank means the Médoc and its communal appellations — Saint-Estèphe, Pauillac, Saint-Julien, Margaux — along with Graves and Pessac-Léognan further south. Cabernet Sauvignon leads, Merlot fills in. These wines are taut and savoury when young, with firm tannin, dark and comparatively cool fruit, and a woody, often cedar-like or pencil-shaving note. They need time, and in their first years they tell you very little about where they are heading.

The right bank means principally Saint-Émilion and Pomerol, plus the satellite appellations and the various Côtes. Merlot leads and Cabernet Franc supplies lift and spice. The texture is rounder, the tannin softer, the fruit riper and darker. The practical point is easy to get wrong: these wines are more approachable early, which does not mean they finish early. The upper tiers live just as long as their left-bank counterparts; they are simply better company along the way.

What the classifications tell you, and what they do not

The 1855 classification ranked Médoc estates and the sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac according to the market prices they were fetching at the time. It ranks properties rather than vineyards and has scarcely changed since. As information it still has some use: an estate on that list has traditionally made wine built for long ageing. As a verdict on the bottle in front of you it has none.

Saint-Émilion runs its own system, revised periodically, with the tiers Grand Cru Classé and Premier Grand Cru Classé. This is where the region's most common confusion sits: Saint-Émilion Grand Cru on its own, without "Classé", is an appellation tier with slightly stricter production rules — not a classification. A great many bottles carry it. Pomerol has no classification at all. The Médoc additionally has the Cru Bourgeois designation, which is reassessed on a regular cycle.

The part where most cellars go wrong

By volume, Bordeaux consists overwhelmingly not of classified estates but of plain appellation wine: Bordeaux AOC and Bordeaux Supérieur. These are made to be drunk. They carry enough fruit for two to five years and hold nothing in reserve beyond that. Leave one for a decade and you do not get a mature Bordeaux, you get a hollowed-out one — the fruit has gone, the tannin has stayed, and what is left tastes dry and angular. Such bottles are rarely faulty. They are simply empty.

The mistake behind it is understandable: "Bordeaux is a wine you lay down" is true of a small minority of the production and then gets applied to the whole region. As a blunt counter-rule, without a communal appellation and the price bracket that goes with it, a Bordeaux was never designed for long ageing, and careful storage will not change that.

White and sweet: entirely different rules

The dry whites from Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon tend to be forgotten. The simple bottlings are fresh wines for one to three years. The barrel- fermented examples, above all from Pessac-Léognan, are among the longest- lived dry whites made anywhere and develop a waxy, nutty depth over many years, usually passing through an unremarkable phase on the way.

Sauternes and Barsac are a category of their own. Botrytis, high sugar and high acidity together make an unusually stable structure, and such wines survive for decades. Their colour travels from pale gold through amber to brown along the way, which is not a fault here — the same colour in a dry white would be a warning. Once opened, a bottle keeps far longer, corked and cold, than any dry wine will.

What you notice in the glass

Still too young: the tannin dries the gums and the palate instead of carrying the wine. The fruit is dark but undifferentiated, and oak sits alongside it as a separate layer. The giveaway is a short finish despite a big impression in the mouth. If the bottle tastes better on the following day, it was opened early.

Ready: the fruit is no longer loud but woven in, and savoury notes arrive around it — cedar, tobacco, undergrowth. The tannin is still present, but it registers as texture rather than resistance.

Past it: the rim in the glass turns brick or orange, the fruit tastes dried rather than fresh, and the middle of the palate goes hollow. Structure and acidity then stand out above whatever fruit remains. These bottles are usually drinkable and rarely faulty, but they no longer deliver what they were laid down for.

Things worth getting right when you lay it down

  • Record the tier, not just the name. With Bordeaux the appellation tier predicts the drinking window better than almost anything else. A list entry that reads only "Bordeaux 2021" has left out the one piece of information that mattered.
  • Take sediment seriously. Older reds throw a noticeable deposit. Stand the bottle upright at least a day before opening and pour off carefully.
  • Large formats age more slowly. With magnums you can push the spans above further out; half bottles arrive earlier and decline earlier too.
  • Check fill level and capsule on any older bottle you buy. The fill level says more about the storage history than any description does, and traces of seepage under the capsule are a warning.
  • With several bottles, open one early and write down what you found. It is the only reliable way to estimate a drinking window, and it only works if the note survives (setting up a cellar is covered separately).

If you take one thing from this: the question with Bordeaux is never "how long does Bordeaux keep", it is "which tier am I holding". A left-bank communal wine and a Bordeaux Supérieur have about as much in common as a single-vineyard bottling and a litre of house red — and if you want a reference point outside France for the stubborn, closed-in youth of the left bank, Nebbiolo is the closest comparison there is.

Ageing estimates are rules of thumb, not guarantees. How long a given bottle carries depends on the vintage, the winemaking and above all on storage. When in doubt, open one bottle earlier than planned.
