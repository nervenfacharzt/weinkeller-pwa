Wachau: Steinfeder, Federspiel, Smaragd — and which of them keeps

In most regions the vineyard name decides how long a wine lasts; in the Wachau it is a single word describing ripeness at harvest.

Last updated: 2026-07-26

At a glance

Country & region
Austria, Lower Austria on the Danube
Key varieties
Grüner Veltliner, Riesling
Character
Dry, taut, often stony and precise
Ageing potential
2 to 20 years, depending on tier

The Wachau is a river valley of roughly thirty kilometres between Melk and Krems, and its defining feature is narrowness. The vineyards sit on terraces built into the slope over centuries, many of them only a few rows wide. That confinement explains much of what ends up in the glass.

Where the character comes from

Geologically the valley splits in two. The steep terraces stand on primary rock — gneiss, amphibolite, weathered stone with very little fine soil between it. Such ground holds almost no water, so vines root deep and berries stay small. On the flatter ground, mainly eastwards towards Loiben and Krems, sits loess: a fine, wind-blown, lime-bearing soil that stores water and gives broader, rounder wines. Riesling has traditionally gone on the rock and Grüner Veltliner more often on loess — a working habit rather than a rule.

The climate is a collision. Warm Pannonian air pushes in from the east, off the Hungarian plain; cool air drains down from the Waldviertel forests to the north and west. The result is a wide gap between day and night temperatures during ripening. Grapes reach aromatic maturity without shedding their acidity, and that ratio — ripe fruit against retained acid — is the whole reason these wines keep. A white wine with no acid has no frame to hold on to over ten years.

The Danube adds its own effect, storing heat and giving it back to the slopes overnight, and the dry stone walls do the same on a smaller scale. In a cool year that saves the harvest. In a hot one it is closer to a liability.

Steinfeder, Federspiel, Smaragd

These three terms are not part of Austrian wine law. They belong to Vinea Wachau, a growers' association whose members also commit to their own code: fruit exclusively from the Wachau, no enrichment, no concentration, no added aromatics. In practice the tiers are alcohol bands, and because the alcohol comes from picking date rather than from the cellar, they are also ripeness bands.

  • Steinfeder — up to 11.5% alcohol. Picked early, light, direct. Named after a feather grass that grows on the terraces.
  • Federspiel — 11.5% to 12.5%. The middle tier: dry, structured, without weight for its own sake.
  • Smaragd — from 12.5% upwards, usually well beyond. Picked late, denser, from the best parcels. Named after the emerald lizard that suns itself on the walls.

For anyone keeping bottles, this is the single most useful thing on the label — more useful than the vineyard name. A Smaragd is not a stronger Federspiel; it is a different type of wine with a different curve over time.

What is worth keeping, and for how long

The spans below are rules of thumb, not promises. Vintage, site and winemaking move them in both directions.

Type Typical drinking window Note
Steinfeder 1 to 3 years Keeping does not improve it, only ages it
Federspiel Grüner Veltliner 2 to 6 years Often gains length in its third year
Federspiel Riesling 3 to 8 years Usually outlasts expectations
Smaragd Grüner Veltliner 5 to 15 years Longer from the best terraces
Smaragd Riesling 8 to 20 years and beyond The longest-lived wine of the region

A Riesling off primary rock develops slowly and in stages. Young, it tastes of citrus, white peach and a near saline edge; after some years honey, dried apricot and the familiar petrol-like note arrive. Nobody has to enjoy that note, but it is a marker of maturity, not of fault. Grüner Veltliner takes a different route: pepper, radish and green apple give way to tobacco, nuts and a broad, almost Burgundian texture.

Signs a bottle is still too young: the wine feels narrow and hard, the alcohol arrives before the fruit, the finish stops short. Smaragd wines also tend to pass through a closed phase, roughly between the second and fourth year after bottling, when the bottle shows less than it cost. Judging one then is judging the wrong thing.

Signs it is going the other way: the colour deepens towards amber, the fruit turns dull and dried, the acid suddenly stands on its own, and the finish shortens with a faint bitterness. Such a wine is not spoiled, but it has stopped giving anything back.

Points to watch when cellaring

Wachau whites are overwhelmingly bottled under screwcap, which has three consequences. They age more slowly and more evenly than wines under cork, so drinking windows quoted in older books tend to be conservative. Cellar humidity is irrelevant to them, and the bottles do not need to lie down. And young examples sometimes show a reductive, flinty or struck-match note, which usually blows off in the glass within twenty minutes.

Light matters more. White wine in pale glass is sensitive to UV, and these bottles have a habit of standing in kitchens rather than in the dark — reason enough to log them and put them away like anything else. It is also worth buying a Smaragd in more than one bottle: the only way to learn a fifteen-year curve is to sample it every few years.

If the style appeals but the prices do not, the related handwriting can be found one valley to the north in the Kamptal — cooler, often spicier, and with keeping qualities that remain widely underrated.

Ageing estimates are rules of thumb, not guarantees. How long a given bottle carries depends on the vintage, the winemaking and above all on storage. When in doubt, open one bottle earlier than planned.
