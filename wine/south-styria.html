South Styria: steep slopes, high acidity, and whites that keep longer than expected

The region has a reputation for young, fresh whites, which is accurate for half of what it makes and thoroughly misleading for the rest.

Last updated: 2026-07-26

At a glance

Country & region
Austria, South Styria on the Slovenian border
Key varieties
Sauvignon Blanc, Morillon, Welschriesling, Gelber Muskateller
Character
Cool-climate whites, taut, with high acidity
Ageing potential
Klassik 1 to 3 years, single vineyard 5 to 20

South Styria is one of the steepest vineyard landscapes in Europe. The slopes drop away sharply enough that most of the work has to be done by hand; terracing is rare, and rows generally run straight down the fall line. That steepness is not a picturesque detail — it is the reason the wines taste the way they do.

Where the character comes from

The climate is Illyrian in influence: damp air from the south, comparatively high rainfall, and pronounced differences between day and night temperatures. Grapes ripen slowly and hold on to acidity that would have burned off elsewhere at the same sugar level. A typical Styrian white therefore has both things at once — ripe aroma and an acid line that keeps the wine upright.

The defining soil is called Opok, a marly, calcareous sandstone that holds water well and releases it slowly, which is an advantage in a region with hot summers. Alongside it there is sand, gravel and, in some sites, more limestone-rich formations. Wines from Opok tend to read tighter and more saline; wines from sand and gravel rounder and more overtly fruity.

Administratively, South Styria is one of three Styrian wine regions, next to Vulkanland Steiermark in the east and Weststeiermark, home of Schilcher — a sharp rosé made from Blauer Wildbacher that has little in common with the whites discussed here and is drunk young as a matter of course.

The main varieties

Sauvignon Blanc is the flagship, and the Styrian style is neither as grassy as New Zealand nor as smoky as the Loire. Young, it smells of elderflower, gooseberry and green pepper; with age the aroma moves towards fennel, quince, herbs and a waxy, smoky note.

Morillon is the local name for Chardonnay. It gives the broadest, creamiest wines of the region, particularly when it sees oak, and it is the second variety — alongside Sauvignon Blanc — whose single-vineyard bottlings are genuinely worth cellaring.

Welschriesling produces the driest, most pared-back wines here: green apple, citrus, very little aromatic display, plenty of acidity. Usually a wine for its first or second summer, though in good sites it is more than a supporting act.

Gelber Muskateller is the most aromatic thing in the range — grapey, lemon zest, elderflower — and almost without exception a wine to drink young. Its appeal is primary aroma, and primary aroma is the first thing to go.

Pinot Blanc, Pinot Gris and Traminer follow, with small amounts of Riesling — although Riesling's real Austrian home lies further north; see Wachau.

Cellaring and drinking windows

The distinction that matters is not the grape but the tier. Almost every Styrian estate works with a pyramid: regional wine, village wine, single-vineyard wine (Ried). A number of growers additionally belong to the STK, a private association that classifies its best sites as Erste STK Lage and Grosse STK Lage.

The Klassik style is built for freshness: stainless steel, little or no wood, early bottling, released in the spring after harvest. These wines gain nothing from waiting. They do not collapse immediately, but the thing you bought them for — the crisp primary fruit — is noticeably paler after two or three years.

Single-vineyard wines are a different proposition: longer lees contact, frequently large oak casks, later bottling and later release. Many estates put their Ried wines on the market a year or two after the Klassik range, and even then they are often not yet where they are heading.

Gelber Muskateller 1 to 2 years Lives on primary aroma, which fades first

Sauvignon Blanc, Klassik 1 to 4 years Second year usually better than the first

Styrian Sauvignon Blanc does age

The common assumption that Sauvignon Blanc is by nature a drink-young variety holds for the light, aromatic style. It does not hold for Styrian single-vineyard wines. What makes them durable is the combination of high acidity, low yields and extended lees ageing. Mature bottles lose the green aromatics almost entirely and gain structure instead: quince, herbs, smoke, an almost oily texture.

Expect a stretch in which the wine gives very little — roughly between its second and fourth year. The primary fruit has gone and the developed aromatics have not yet arrived. Opening a bottle in that phase and being disappointed says nothing about the rest of the case.

Reading it in the glass

Too young: the wine smells closed or distinctly reductive — flint, struck match, sometimes a trace of onion that blows off within minutes. The acidity sits sharp and separate, the mid-palate feels narrow, and any oak stands as its own layer beside the fruit. A wine that improves markedly over an hour in an open glass is telling you it still has years in hand.

Mature: a deep yellow-gold colour, an aroma that is broader and quieter, acidity that carries rather than pierces, and texture as the most striking thing about the wine.

Fading: the colour drifts towards brown, the aroma moves to baked apple, honey or sherry-like notes, and a hole opens between the attack and the acidity. The acid itself survives a long time in Styrian wines, which is why they taste hollow rather than flabby on the way down.

What to watch when you shelve them

  • Record the tier. "Sauvignon Blanc 2024" is not usable information without Klassik, village or Ried attached; the drinking windows differ by a factor of several.
  • Light is the bigger risk with whites. Many Styrian wines come in pale glass, where lightstrike develops faster than in dark bottles. Keeping them boxed or in a dark room is not excessive caution.
  • Note the closure. Screwcaps are widespread in Austria and let wine mature more slowly and more reductively. Those bottles often benefit from decanting more than corked ones do.
  • Do not collect Klassik. These wines were bought to be drunk. Anything still lying there after three years was forgotten, not aged.

On keeping drinking windows visible on the shelf in the first place, see setting up a wine cellar.

Ageing estimates are rules of thumb, not guarantees. How long a given bottle carries depends on the vintage, the winemaking and above all on storage. When in doubt, open one bottle earlier than planned.
