Pinot Noir: the thin-skinned grape and its awkward ageing curve

Little colour, little tannin, plenty of acidity — and still one of the longest-lived red grapes. That contradiction explains nearly everything that matters in a cellar.

Last updated: 2026-07-26

At a glance

Origin
Burgundy, France
Key regions
Burgundy, Germany, Austria, New Zealand, Oregon
Character
Pale, perfumed, acid-driven, low in colour and tannin
Ageing potential
2 to 25 years depending on origin and tier

Pinot Noir has a reputation for being difficult, and for once the reputation is earned. The grape has thin skins, grows in tight, compact bunches and ripens early. Each of those traits is a liability in the vineyard: thin skins mean vulnerability to rot and to rain before harvest, tight bunches mean poor air circulation, early ripening means the picking window can be missed. In Germany and Austria the same grape appears as Spätburgunder or Blauburgunder, in Italy as Pinot Nero. It is always the same plant.

Why site and vintage show through so clearly

Everything else follows from the thin skins. Colour and tannin live in the skin, so where there is little skin there is little of either. Pinot Noir is consequently pale in the glass and soft in the mouth, and it lacks the thick coat of tannin and extract behind which sturdier varieties can hide a mediocre vintage or a rough decision in the cellar. Whatever is in the bottle is on display. That applies to the grower's hand, to the site and above all to the weather of the year.

Sensitivity to heat compounds it. Pinot Noir needs cool conditions; in a climate that is too warm it loses precisely what people drink it for — perfume, clarity and lift — and turns jammy and heavy instead. The map of serious Pinot regions is therefore a map of cool niches: north-facing slopes, altitude, proximity to cold water, fog.

The main homes, one sentence each

  • Burgundy: the reference point, with a finely graded hierarchy running from regional wine through village and premier cru sites to the grands crus, and with the widest gap between tiers that this grape produces anywhere.
  • Germany: Spätburgunder, chiefly from Baden, the Pfalz and the Ahr, now mostly dry and barrel-aged, ranging stylistically from fruit-forward to consciously Burgundian.
  • Austria: Blauburgunder in modest quantities, centred on Burgenland, Lower Austria and Styria, usually slender and acid-driven rather than weighty.
  • New Zealand: Central Otago and Martinborough above all, with Marlborough alongside — firm dark fruit, clear structure and considerable charm when young.
  • Oregon: the Willamette Valley, cool and wet, giving medium-bodied wines with marked acidity and earthy, savoury notes.
  • California: the cool fog-influenced zones — Sonoma Coast, Russian River Valley, Santa Barbara — with riper fruit and higher alcohol than the European models.

Ageing: low tannin, long life

The familiar rule of thumb that tannin equals longevity is misleading here. Tannin is only one preservative among several, and acidity is the second — which Pinot Noir has in quantity. Good sites add a high density of extract despite the pale colour. That is why bottles that look delicate in the glass outlive heavier, darker wines from other grapes. The reverse also holds: a simple Pinot without that density is a drinking wine for two to four years and never becomes anything else. Variety tells you even less about longevity here than it does with Bordeaux.

The real peculiarity is the closed phase. Serious Pinot is often disarming when young: open red fruit, perfume, a soft texture. Then it disappears, commonly somewhere between the third and the eighth year. The wine goes mute, the fruit flattens and turns slightly metallic, the acidity stands exposed, and more than one owner concludes the bottle is faulty. It almost never is. The wine comes back afterwards with a different set of aromas: dried cherry, forest floor, mushroom, tea, sometimes something gamey. Knowing the shape of the curve is what stops people from writing off the rest of a case halfway through it.

What you notice in the glass

Still too young: the perfume is there but narrow and one-note, usually red fruit with nothing around it. Oak and fruit sit beside each other rather than within each other. The acidity leads, and the tannin feels fine-grained and faintly rough along the edges of the tongue. A useful test: if the wine shows considerably more after two hours in the glass than it did on pouring, it still has something in reserve.

In the closed phase: a short, almost silent nose, flat fruit, a lean mouthfeel. Unlike a wine past its peak, nothing is actually missing — it simply is not showing. The right response is two or three more years of patience, not decanting.

Past its peak: the colour is pale with a brick to brown rim, the nose moves to dried fruit and earth, and what stays in the mouth is mainly acidity. With Pinot Noir this tips over faster than with tannic varieties. The narrow window at the end is the price of the delicacy at the beginning.

Things worth getting right when you lay it down

Pinot Noir forgives poor storage less readily than robust varieties. Pigments and tannins also act as a buffer against oxygen inside the bottle, and where there is less of them, any oxygen ingress shows up sooner. The same corner of a cellar in which a big red survives a decade untouched can strip a Pinot of its freshness in five years.

  • Steady temperature above everything else. The absolute reading matters less than the swing. With Pinot Noir it is worth reserving the quietest, most even spot in the cellar for these bottles (the basics are covered here).
  • Lie the bottles down if they are under natural cork. A cork that dries out costs more here than with any other grape, because there is less substance in the wine to mask the damage.
  • Do not serve it too cold. Below about 14 °C (57 °F) the aromatics you cellared it for vanish; much above 18 °C (64 °F) the wine turns broad and alcoholic.
  • Aerate cautiously. Mature Pinot often reacts badly to a long decant and fades in the glass faster than a full-bodied wine. Open the bottle and watch it in the glass instead.
  • Record the vintage, not just grower and grape. With scarcely any other red do two adjacent vintages of the same wine differ so much.

If you take one thing from this: with Pinot Noir a drinking window is not a straight line from young to old but a curve with a hole in the middle. Plan for the hole, open one bottle early as a probe, and you avoid the most common disappointment of all — a good bottle opened at the wrong moment and remembered ever after as a bad purchase.

Ageing estimates are rules of thumb, not guarantees. How long a given bottle carries depends on the vintage, the winemaking and above all on storage. When in doubt, open one bottle earlier than planned.
