Blaufränkisch: dark fruit, firm acidity and three Burgenland signatures

Few red varieties wear their origin this openly, and few stretch this far from litre bottle to single-vineyard wine.

Last updated: 2026-07-26

At a glance

Origin
Central Europe, centred on Austria and western Hungary
Key regions
Burgenland, Sopron, Württemberg
Character
Dark berries, high acidity, firm tannin
Ageing potential
1 to 15 years depending on style

Blaufränkisch is the variety that teaches Austrian drinkers that origin can be tasted. Two bottles of the same vintage, both single-varietal, both dry, can differ so much that you would hesitate to put them down to the same grape in a blind tasting. That is not chance, and it is not only the producer's hand. This vine passes soil and climate along with unusually little filtering.

One variety, three names

In Hungary it is Kékfrankos, in Germany — chiefly in Württemberg — Lemberger or Blauer Limberger, in Slovakia and Croatia Frankovka. Same vine throughout. If you have bottles under several of these names, treat them as one group in your records; they behave similarly over the years, although German bottlings tend on average to be rounder and approachable earlier than Burgenland's.

Blaufränkisch is a natural cross and itself a parent of several younger varieties, among them Zweigelt. In the vineyard it is demanding. It buds early, which exposes it to spring frost, and ripens late, which demands warm sites with reliable water. That combination explains why it only really convinces in a narrow belt of central Europe.

What happens in the glass

The fruit is dark and tart rather than sweet: blackberry, sour cherry, dark cherry, and in ripe years plum. There is nearly always a savoury edge alongside it — black pepper, sometimes something herbal, and in the cooler origins a pull towards smoke.

Two structural traits matter more than the aromas. First the acidity, which stays high even in hot years; it is the reason Blaufränkisch rarely turns broad or jammy. Second the tannin, which is firm but fine-grained. Young bottles therefore often seem more approachable than they are — the structure only announces itself in the finish. Alcohol usually sits at moderate levels, which makes the variety easy company at the table.

Three Burgenland signatures

By far the largest share of Austria's plantings sits in Burgenland, and there the variety separates into three clearly distinguishable characters. Anyone who has tasted them side by side reads labels differently afterwards.

Mittelburgenland, known as Blaufränkischland for good reason: heavy loam soils, sheltered position, warm. The wines are dense, dark and powerful, with noticeable oak in the upper tiers. They impress young and grow finer over the years.

Leithaberg, on the hills north-west of Lake Neusiedl: limestone and mica schist, more altitude, cooler nights. The result is leaner and tauter, often with an almost saline note on the finish. Young, these wines can feel austere, occasionally unwelcoming; they need more patience than their weight suggests.

Eisenberg in southern Burgenland, on iron-bearing schist: finely drawn, spicy, with pronounced acidity and a smoky, mineral tone that many regard as the variety's most distinctive expression. These wines drink well earlier, but the acidity carries them further than their frame implies.

Immediately across the border, around Sopron, the same vine grows under comparable conditions as Kékfrankos; it also plays a load-bearing role in Eger and Szekszárd, often as part of a blend. More on the Austrian regions is in the Burgenland profile.

The styles and their timeframes

For cellar purposes the decisive category is not the variety but the type of wine. The ranges below are rules of thumb and should be read as ranges.

Style How to recognise it Typical range from the vintage
Classic, steel or large oak Variety name alone, low price, often a litre bottle roughly 1 to 4 years
Regional or village wine Origin named on the label, some used barriques roughly 3 to 8 years
Single vineyard or Reserve Named Ried, longer cask ageing, the estate's dearest bottle roughly 5 to 15 years
Blend with a Blaufränkisch share Proprietary name, varieties listed in the small print roughly 3 to 10 years

A Blaufränkisch is still too young when the fruit stands there loud and one-dimensional, the oak sits beside it as a separate layer, and the finish breaks off short despite a powerful mouthfeel. It has been kept too long when the rim turns brick brown, the fruit tastes stewed, and the acidity is left standing on its own without fruit around it. Because the ranges here are unusually wide, that question has a piece of its own: when is Blaufränkisch ready to drink?

Serving and food

Sixteen to eighteen degrees is the sensible band; warmer than that and the alcohol steps forward while the acidity turns angular. Light, fruit-driven bottlings will even take cellar temperature, around fourteen to fifteen degrees. Young single-vineyard wines almost always gain from an hour in a decanter, whereas older bottles with sediment want only a careful pour off the deposit.

At the table the acidity is the advantage. Blaufränkisch copes with rich, strongly seasoned food where softer reds give up: goulash, braised meat, game, roast duck, mature mountain cheese. The simpler styles suit cold cuts and anything off the grill. What does not work is genuine sweetness — the acidity then reads as sour.

What to watch when laying it down

The good news first: Blaufränkisch is robust. Acidity and tannin forgive an imperfect cellar more readily than soft, low-tannin reds do. Three points still deserve attention.

Record the style, not just the variety. “Blaufränkisch 2023” tells you nothing in five years' time; “Blaufränkisch 2023, single vineyard, barrique” tells you everything. If you log only the grape, you will later face bottles whose window you can no longer estimate.

For single-vineyard wines, buy more than one bottle. The curve across ten years can only be learned by sampling it every few years. One bottle opened early, with the result written down, is worth more than any recommendation.

Reserve bottlings usually come under natural cork. The usual conditions therefore apply: store them lying down, sixty to eighty per cent humidity, no sudden temperature swings. Simple wines under screwcap are independent of all that — and they are the bottles that should not be waiting anyway.

Ageing estimates are rules of thumb, not guarantees. How long a given bottle carries depends on the vintage, the winemaking and above all on storage. When in doubt, open one bottle earlier than planned.
