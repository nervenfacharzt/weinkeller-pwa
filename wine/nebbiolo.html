Nebbiolo: pale in the glass, formidable in the mouth

No other red variety misleads so completely — judge one by its colour and the first mouthful will catch you unprepared.

Last updated: 2026-07-26

At a glance

Origin
Piedmont, north-west Italy
Key regions
Langhe, Roero, Alto Piemonte, Valtellina
Character
Pale, high acid, formidable tannin
Ageing potential
3 to 25 years, depending on origin

Hold the glass up to the light and you see something pale and almost see-through, with an orange rim, and brace for something delicate. What arrives instead dries the tongue, pulls the inside of the cheeks together and carries an arc of acidity that runs on for a full minute. That gap between appearance and effect is the first thing to know about Nebbiolo, and it accounts for most of the disappointment people have with the variety.

What defines the grape

Nebbiolo has thin skins and little colouring matter, yet an unusually large tannin load. Having both at once is rare: in most red varieties depth of colour and tannin rise more or less together. Not here. On top of that sits high acidity that survives even warm vintages. A wine with plenty of tannin and plenty of acid has two frames rather than one, which is the technical basis of its longevity and equally the reason it can feel so forbidding when young.

The vine buds early and ripens late, so it needs a long season and ties itself to cool places that can nonetheless bring it to full ripeness. Only a few corners of north-west Italy manage that, and outside Italy the variety has never really taken hold. The name is usually linked to nebbia, the fog that settles over the Piedmontese hills in October when the fruit comes in.

Aromatically it occupies its own territory: rose and violet, dark cherry, tar, aniseed, dried herbs. Years later come leather, undergrowth, tobacco and, in properly mature bottles, a truffled note. The colour fades early — a brick-red rim after five years is normal for this variety, not a sign of damage.

Where it works

The best-known home is the Langhe south of Alba, where calcareous marls give Barolo and Barbaresco. They set the benchmark, but for a working cellar the other origins are often more practical.

Roero sits across the Tanaro on distinctly sandier ground. Its wines are more perfumed, finer-boned and softer in tannin, and they open years earlier. Langhe Nebbiolo is not a site but a broad appellation under which estates bottle younger vines, outlying parcels or fruit declassified from their senior wines, which makes it the sensible way in: same hand, same grape, a fraction of the waiting.

In the Alto Piemonte to the north-east the variety is called Spanna. Gattinara and Ghemme are the familiar names, alongside Boca, Bramaterra, Lessona, Fara and Sizzano. Soils here are acidic and partly volcanic in origin, the climate cooler and alpine in feel. The wines come across as leaner, herbier and more saline than those of the Langhe, and small proportions of other local varieties are traditionally blended in.

The second major home is not in Piedmont at all. In Lombardy's Valtellina the grape goes by Chiavennasca and clings to steep terraces on the north side of an east-west valley. Besides the dry wines of the Sassella, Grumello, Inferno and Valgella subzones, there is Sforzato, made from grapes dried before fermentation: denser, warmer and higher in alcohol, on the same acid frame.

Traditional and modern

A second distinction has nothing to do with place. The classical school favours long macerations and large, well-used casks; those wines are austere in youth and demand time. The more modern approach uses gentler extraction and sometimes smaller barrels, giving wines that drink earlier. Both routes produce wine that keeps, but not along the same curve.

Cellaring and drinking windows

Nebbiolo is the textbook case of the bottle opened too soon: in no other variety is the gap so wide between what a bottle holds and what a premature mouthful shows of it. The spans below are rules of thumb; vintage, site and cellar style move them in both directions.

Type Typical drinking window Note
Langhe Nebbiolo 2 to 6 years The practical way in, often open in its second year
Nebbiolo d'Alba, Roero 3 to 8 years Sandy soils make the tannin approachable sooner
Valtellina Superiore 5 to 15 years Slighter on entry, still keeps for years
Gattinara, Ghemme and neighbours 8 to 20 years Routinely underestimated
Barolo, Barbaresco 8 to 25 years and beyond Riserva bottlings correspondingly longer

Signs a bottle is still too young: the tannin arrives as a dry, dusty sheet in front of the fruit and coats the whole palate rather than settling at the back. The acid feels pointed and detached from everything else. The wine smells of little while doing a great deal in the mouth. If the glass improves markedly over two hours, nothing is wrong with it — it is simply early. Many Langhe wines also pass through a closed phase some years after bottling, showing less then than they did on release.

Signs it is fading: the fruit thins towards dried fruit while the tannin stays put, leaving a wine that is hard with nothing behind it. The nose turns earthy and musty rather than spicy, and the palate drops away after the attack instead of carrying. Colour is no help here, because it went brick-red long before.

Points to watch when cellaring

Nebbiolo throws a reliable deposit with age. Bottles due to be opened should therefore stand upright for a day or two so the sediment can settle. Decanting is not ceremony with this variety but housekeeping: off the deposit, and into the air — with young bottles, an hour or two before the meal.

These wines come almost exclusively under natural cork, so the usual rules apply: bottles on their side, middling humidity, and above all a steady temperature. For anything meant to lie ten years or more, temperature stability matters more than any other variable, a point covered in the piece on setting up a cellar.

If a wine is meant to carry twenty years, buy more than one bottle. The only way to learn its curve is to sample it every few years, and the mistake most often made with Nebbiolo is the one that cannot be undone: opening the only bottle too early. If the structure appeals, the related hand can be found in Sangiovese — similarly driven by acidity, but far more accommodating in its tannin.

Ageing estimates are rules of thumb, not guarantees. How long a given bottle carries depends on the vintage, the winemaking and above all on storage. When in doubt, open one bottle earlier than planned.
