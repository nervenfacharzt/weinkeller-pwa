Sangiovese: one grape, many wines, very different windows

The same grape sits behind a simple Rosso and a Brunello — separated by a decade of patience.

Last updated: 2026-07-31

At a glance

Origin
Central Italy, above all Tuscany
Key regions
Chianti Classico, Montalcino, Montepulciano, Maremma
Character
Sour cherry, high acid, grippy tannin
Ageing potential
2 to 25 years, depending on tier

Sangiovese is Italy's most widely planted red variety, and that is part of the difficulty. Meet it through its most famous wines and it seems austere and tannic. Meet it through a straightforward Rosso and it seems easy and fruity. Both impressions are accurate. The grape responds to site, clone and yield more sharply than almost any other, which is why the name on its own says very little about how long a bottle should stay in the rack.

What defines the grape

The common thread is easy enough to describe: sour cherry, red plum, a pronounced herbal note somewhere near dried oregano or thyme, sometimes tomato leaf and black tea. On the palate, high and clear acidity, and a tannin that is not heavy but grippy and makes itself felt along the gums. The colour is medium ruby and turns garnet briskly with age. Sangiovese is rarely thick and always more savoury than sweet, which is exactly where its usefulness at the table comes from.

The vine has thin skins, ripens late and dislikes damp weather at harvest. It also crops generously if allowed to, and at high yields it turns thin and green. Layered on top is an unusually wide range of clones and regional strains — Sangiovese Grosso at Montalcino, Prugnolo Gentile at Montepulciano, Morellino in the Maremma. Serious clonal selection has been under way for decades, aimed at smaller berries and more stable colour, and it explains much of the step up in quality over the past generation.

The principal styles

Chianti Classico is the classical face of the variety: hill country between Florence and Siena, Sangiovese as the dominant grape with limited proportions of others, arranged in the tiers Annata, Riserva and Gran Selezione. The wines are herbal, taut and made for the table. Subzone names have recently begun appearing on labels, meant to show the considerable differences within the zone.

Brunello di Montalcino comes from a warmer, drier zone further south and is made from Sangiovese alone. The wines are denser, more tannic and markedly longer-lived. The rules require extended ageing before release, a substantial part of it in wood, so a Brunello cannot be sold until several years after the harvest. Rosso di Montalcino comes from the same ground, often the same estates, but reaches the market far sooner.

Vino Nobile di Montepulciano sits between the two: Prugnolo Gentile as the base, other varieties permitted alongside, mandatory cask ageing but less of it than at Montalcino. Here too there is an earlier-drinking counterpart in Rosso di Montepulciano.

Morellino di Scansano lies on the coast in the Maremma. Warmer, lower, riper in fruit and softer in tannin, it is the most immediately likeable of the classical Sangiovese styles and the one that least needs keeping. Sangiovese is also the leading red of Romagna, part of the Carmignano blend alongside Cabernet, and present in countless Tuscan bottlings below the senior appellations.

For cellaring purposes the important division is not Chianti against Brunello but senior wine against second wine. The Rosso bottlings are built to be drunk early; keeping them does not turn them into small Brunelli, only into older ones.

Cellaring and drinking windows

The range within a single variety is remarkable — from a Rosso that wants drinking within two years to a Brunello from a structured vintage that needs a decade before it becomes interesting. The figures below are rules of thumb, not promises.

Type Typical drinking window Note
Rosso di Montalcino, Rosso di Montepulciano 2 to 5 years Made as an early-drinking wine
Morellino di Scansano 2 to 6 years Warmer origin, softer tannin
Chianti Classico Annata 2 to 6 years Often gains in its third or fourth year
Chianti Classico Riserva and Gran Selezione 5 to 20 years Longer from the better sites
Vino Nobile di Montepulciano 5 to 12 years Riserva correspondingly longer
Brunello di Montalcino 8 to 25 years Riserva and strong vintages beyond that

What happens to the aromatics over time is characteristic of the grape. The sour cherry does not vanish overnight, but it recedes, and leather, tobacco, dried herbs, undergrowth and sometimes dried orange peel move into its place. The acidity meanwhile stays remarkably firm, which is why mature Sangiovese rarely tastes tired as long as there is substance left underneath.

Signs a bottle is still too young: the wine feels narrow and pointed, the acid arrives ahead of the fruit, the tannin is dry and scratchy rather than grainy, and the herbal note tips over into something green and stalky. Brunello and the better Riservas also tend to pass through a phase some years after bottling when they close up and say little.

Signs it is fading: the fruit has gone entirely, leaving acidity and a dry, woody finish. The nose smells of dust and old paper rather than herbs and leather. At the rim the colour turns brown rather than garnet, and the finish stops early. Such a bottle is not spoiled, but it has stopped giving anything back.

Points to watch when cellaring

Sangiovese is almost always bottled under natural cork, and usually bought long before anyone opens it. The usual rules therefore apply with extra force: bottles on their side, steady temperature, no daylight nearby. Anyone planning to hold Brunello for ten years should take the storage location seriously; the practical side is covered in the piece on setting up a cellar.

The long-lived styles throw a dependable deposit, though less than Nebbiolo does. Stand older bottles upright a day ahead and pour carefully. Young, tannic examples benefit from an hour of air; with genuinely old bottles the opposite holds — open them shortly before drinking and watch them develop in the glass.

The commonest mistake, though, is not opening at the wrong moment but expecting the wrong thing from the tier. A Rosso kept three years too long loses precisely what it was bought for; a Brunello opened three years too early shows precisely nothing of it. Both are avoidable if what gets written down at shelving is not only the name but the tier — Rosso, Annata, Riserva, Gran Selezione. With few other varieties does one word carry so much.

Ageing estimates are rules of thumb, not guarantees. How long a given bottle carries depends on the vintage, the winemaking and above all on storage. When in doubt, open one bottle earlier than planned.
