Working out a drinking window: when a bottle is ready

Nothing tells you the maturity of a sealed bottle from the outside. There are only good estimates — and one method that improves them every year.

Last updated: 2026-07-31

The most expensive bottle in a cellar is rarely the one stored too warm. It is the one that was forgotten. Wine does not spoil abruptly; it goes quiet, until one day nothing is left of what you bought it for. A drinking window is an attempt to anticipate that moment, and it is the one entry in an inventory that cannot be copied off the label.

What a drinking window is, and is not

A window has two edges and a middle. The early edge is the point at which a wine starts showing what it was made for: the tannin no longer stands in front of the fruit, the oak has been absorbed, the acidity carries the wine instead of running alongside it. The late edge arrives when the fruit has receded far enough that only structure is left — acidity and grip with nothing around them.

What lies between is not a peak but a plateau. That is the important correction to the common picture: wines do not climb towards a single perfect day and fall away after it. They reach a state in which they are good for years, and they leave it slowly. Reading a window of “8 to 20 years” does not mean you have to hit year 14. It means avoiding year 3 — and avoiding the discovery, in year 25, that you waited too long.

One more thing belongs here: a window describes a bottle, not a wine. Two bottles of the same vintage, stored differently, can sit years apart.

Where the length comes from

Four things carry a wine through time, and alcohol on its own is not one of them.

Acidity is the most reliable. It breaks down only slowly in the bottle and holds a wine upright while the aromatics rearrange themselves. This is why light, high-acid whites often outlast heavy, soft reds — a fact that surprises people regularly.

Tannin buffers a red wine against oxygen and softens over the years, eventually dropping out as sediment. It guarantees nothing on its own: a wine with plenty of tannin and little fruit does not mature, it merely turns hard.

Sugar preserves. That is why botrytis sweet wines have the longest windows of all — with a Trockenbeerenauslese you count in decades, not years.

Extract — everything dissolved beyond water and alcohol — is the least visible factor, and it explains why wines from small yields last longer than wines from large ones. It appears on no label; price is its best proxy.

Estimating without a tasting note

Faced with an unfamiliar bottle, four features will take you surprisingly far. None carries on its own; together they give a usable picture.

Feature Suggests a short window Suggests a long one
Origin tier Only a grape or a region on the label Single vineyard, village, Reserve, Riserva
Maturation Stainless steel, large old wood Small barrels, some new oak, long time on the lees
Release Sold the spring after harvest Appears two or three years after the vintage
Price within the estate The entry-level bottle The most expensive wine the estate makes

The rule of thumb behind all four is crude but dependable: an estate's cheapest wine is meant for drinking, its dearest for waiting. For how this plays out variety by variety, the profiles carry the ranges — see Nebbiolo, where twenty years separate the entry level from the top, or Grüner Veltliner, whose single-vineyard wines are routinely underestimated.

The closed phase

This is where most estimates come apart. Many serious wines pass through a stretch in which they show less than they did shortly after release. The youthful fruit has gone; the mature notes have not arrived. The bottle tastes of considerably less than it cost.

It is not a fault but a passage, and it affects very different wines: Pinot Noir often between years three and eight, Grüner Veltliner and Styrian single-vineyard whites between two and four, Barolo some years after bottling. Anyone who tastes during that stretch and comes away disappointed almost always draws the wrong conclusion — namely to drink the rest quickly.

The only dependable method

No table replaces what an open bottle tells you. If you own three or six bottles of the same wine, open one early — noticeably earlier than feels right — and write down what happened. Not as a score, but as an observation: how did the tannin sit? Was the oak absorbed? And above all: how did the wine change over two hours in the glass?

That last question is the most informative one you can ask a bottle. If the wine gains clearly over an hour or two, it has reserves in hand, and two to three years until the next bottle is a sensible call. If it fades after an hour, it is at or past its point, and the remaining bottles belong to the coming year. A second signal comes from the leftovers the next day: if they taste better than they did the evening before, the bottle was opened too early.

What shifts a window

  • Storage. Not the absolute temperature but the swing. A wine kept steadily at sixteen degrees ages more predictably than one moving between eight and eighteen — more on that in the guide to setting up a wine cellar.
  • Bottle size. Halves mature faster, magnums slower. The ratio of wine to the air in the neck decides it, and at 375 ml that ratio is at its least favourable.
  • The closure. Under screwcap, wine ages more slowly and more evenly than under natural cork. Ranges quoted in older literature tend to be too short for such bottles.
  • The vintage. With some varieties, two neighbouring vintages of the same wine sit years apart. Which is why the year belongs in the note, not just the grower and the wine.

What to write down

The part where this whole exercise usually fails is the writing down. Without a record, the bottle you opened early is forgotten within two years and you are back where you started. Three things are enough: a rough span (“from 2030” will do), the style in a single word (single vineyard, Reserve, Klassik, Annata), and the result of the trial bottle in one sentence.

If you take one thing from this: a roughly guessed drinking window is infinitely better than none. The mistake that costs something is not the imprecise estimate — it is the missing one.
