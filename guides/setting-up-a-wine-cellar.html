Setting up a wine cellar: temperature, order and a system that lasts

The physics takes five minutes to explain. The harder part comes afterwards: settling on an order you will still understand years later.

Last updated: 2026-07-26

A wine cellar is not a storeroom. It is a brake. Everything that happens in it serves one purpose: to slow down the chemistry inside the bottle and keep it running evenly. That is why the same four factors come up every time — and why one of them matters more than the other three put together.

The four factors, in order of importance

1. A steady temperature beats the right temperature

The textbook range is 10 to 14 °C (50 to 57 °F). What matters more in practice is the movement. A room that sits at a stable 16 °C (61 °F) all year is better than one that swings between 8 °C (46 °F) in February and 18 °C (64 °F) in August. Wine expands when it warms and contracts when it cools, and every cycle pushes a little air past the cork. The heat itself is not really the problem; the cycling is.

As a rule of thumb: slow seasonal drift is survivable. Daily swings are not. A cupboard next to a boiler that fires twice a day is worse than an evenly cool utility room, even if the average reading looks identical.

2. Humidity: 60 to 80 per cent

Too dry and the cork shrinks, which lets the bottle breathe. Too damp and the labels grow mould, which does nothing to the wine but a great deal to its resale value. Anyone with an unfinished cellar is usually in range already. In a dry room, an open bowl of water is often enough — in a small space it works surprisingly well, and it costs nothing to try before you buy a humidifier.

Bottles under screwcap or a glass stopper are indifferent to humidity, and they do not need to lie down either.

3. Darkness

UV light breaks down the compounds that aroma is built from. White wine in clear glass is the most vulnerable, and the fault has its own name: lightstrike. This does not mean you need a blacked-out room. No daylight and no permanent lighting is enough; a lamp you switch on when you walk in is entirely fine.

4. Quiet

Vibration keeps the sediment moving and stops a wine from settling. It only becomes relevant for bottles that lie for years, which is why it sits last on this list. The usual mistake in a flat is the obvious one: the rack stands on or beside the fridge or the washing machine.

What people overestimate — and what they underestimate

The need for a real cellar is overestimated. A north-facing store cupboard, a wardrobe in an unheated room, an insulated corner of a loft — all of them work, as long as the temperature does not jump. If you are thinking about air conditioning, put a min/max thermometer in the room for a fortnight first. It costs almost nothing and answers the question better than any recommendation can. If you have no suitable room at all, the trade-offs are different enough to be worth their own article: storing wine without a cellar.

What almost everyone underestimates is order. Somewhere around fifty bottles something tips over. You still know that you own a particular wine, but not where it is. Past a hundred, you start finding bottles whose drinking window closed two years ago. At that point the losses have nothing to do with storage conditions. They come from forgetting.

A system you will still understand in five years

There are three common organising principles. None of them is wrong, but they fail in different ways.

Principle Strength Weakness
By region or producer Intuitive to search when you already know what you want Readiness becomes invisible — old and young vintages sit side by side
By drinking window Nothing gets forgotten; “what is ready now” is simply a shelf You have to sort as you put bottles away, and bottles migrate over time
By date laid down No decisions when stocking — the new bottle goes at the front Without a list you find nothing at all

In practice a hybrid works best: store bottles physically wherever it takes the least effort (usually: wherever there is space) and move the actual order into a list. The rack then becomes nothing more than storage, and the question “what shall we open tonight?” is answered by looking something up rather than by searching.

If you stay with paper or a spreadsheet, keep at least these fields: producer, wine, vintage, quantity, location, drinking window. Location is the field almost everyone leaves out and later wishes they had. The drinking window is the field that turns an inventory into a tool — even a rough estimate works, and for grapes you buy often it is worth learning the pattern once, as with Blaufränkisch and its ageing curve.

The first fifty bottles: an order of operations

  1. Measure for two weeks. Min/max thermometer in the room in question. Decide whether it is suitable only after you have the numbers.
  2. Buy a rack; do not stack boxes. Cardboard is a temporary solution that lasts three years and inside which nothing is ever found again. A plain wooden or metal rack with fixed compartments is enough.
  3. Name the positions before you fill them. Rows A to D, columns 1 to 6 is plenty. All that matters is that a position has a name you can write down.
  4. Record bottles as you shelve them, not afterwards. Nobody ever goes back and catalogues fifty bottles retrospectively.
  5. Write down the drinking window from the start. Roughly is fine. “From 2028” is infinitely better than nothing.

The mistake that costs the most

It is not storing wine too warm. It is the bottle you held on to until it was too old, because nothing reminded you. A wine three years past its window is rarely undrinkable, but it is no longer the thing you bought. And this happens to well-stocked cellars without exception, never to small ones — the more you own, the more reliably something slips through.

If you take one thing from this: record the location and the drinking window. Everything else is comfort.
