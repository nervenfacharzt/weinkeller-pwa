How long does opened wine last? By style, with the remedies that work

The half bottle left from last night is not an edge case but the normal case — and how you handle it decides more evenings than any cellar question.

Last updated: 2026-07-31

Two people, one bottle, a weeknight: a third of it is still there at the end. What happens to that remainder is a question that comes up far more often than any storage question — and it is usually answered with a single wrong number, because the answer depends heavily on the style.

What is actually happening

Two processes run in parallel, and only one of them is reversible.

The first is oxidation. Oxygen alters the aroma compounds: freshness goes, the fruit turns dull and drifts towards baked apple, nuts or sherry, and in red wine the rim of the glass browns. In small doses this is exactly what you want — it is why a young wine tastes better after an hour in a decanter. In larger doses it is the end.

The second is microbiological. Acetic acid bacteria need oxygen and convert alcohol into acetic acid. This takes longer than oxidation, but it is final: a wine that smells of vinegar or solvent does not come back.

Both are governed by the same three variables: how much air there is relative to wine in the bottle, how warm it is, and what protection the wine brings of its own — acidity, tannin, sugar and alcohol all preserve. Which is why a taut Riesling outlasts a soft red, however counterintuitive that sounds.

How long each style holds

These figures assume a re-sealed bottle in the fridge, and they mean “still tastes good”, not “still drinkable”. Drinkable lasts considerably longer.

Style Realistic What goes first
Sparkling wine 1 to 3 days The bubbles, long before the aroma
Light white, rosé 2 to 4 days The primary fruit; the acidity stays
Full-bodied, oak-aged white 3 to 5 days Freshness; the texture carries longer
Light red, low tannin 2 to 3 days The fruit flattens and turns dull
Tannic red 3 to 5 days Often better on day two than on day one
Very old red A few hours Everything at once — this one is for tonight
Botrytis sweet wine 1 to 3 weeks Very little; sugar and acidity protect it
Fortified (port, sherry, madeira) 2 weeks to months Depends on the type; oloroso and madeira barely change

Two rows in that table surprise people regularly. The first is the very old red: a bottle that has lain for twenty years can fall apart in the glass within the hour. What has already matured for a long time has spent its reserves. Open those when there are enough people at the table to finish them.

The second is the tannic red that tastes better on the second day. This does not contradict the oxidation story; it is the same thing in a small dose — a young, closed wine benefits from air. If you notice it, incidentally, it tells you something about the bottle: it was still too young (how to work out a drinking window).

What helps, and what does not

Put it in the fridge. By far the most effective single measure, and it applies to red wine exactly as much as to white. Cold slows both processes markedly. Take the red out half an hour before you pour; that is the entire effort involved.

Decant it into a smaller bottle. The second most effective measure and the one people are least likely to bother with. Half a litre of wine in a 750 ml bottle sits under a lot of air; the same wine filled to the neck of a 500 ml bottle sits under almost none. A rinsed-out water bottle with a screwcap does the job perfectly.

Put the closure back. Obvious, but worth saying: the bottle then stands up rather than lying down. On its side, the surface where wine meets air is larger.

Vacuum pumps are the classic and do less than their reputation suggests. They remove some of the air, do not hold the partial vacuum for long, and take volatile aroma compounds out along with the air. On sparkling wine they are worse than useless: a vacuum pulls the dissolved carbon dioxide out of the wine. A gain of one to two days is realistic; more is not.

Inert gas — argon or nitrogen from a spray can — is the most effective of the technical solutions. The gas is heavier than air and settles as a blanket on the wine. If you regularly drink single glasses from expensive bottles it earns its keep; for the everyday bottle it is effort for a benefit that decanting into something smaller delivers nearly as well.

The teaspoon in the neck of the champagne bottle does nothing. This has been measured. What works is a proper sparkling wine stopper with a clamp — and the fridge, because cold liquid holds its carbon dioxide better.

Telling when it is over

The smell decides, not the date. A wine is past it when the fruit reads flat and dull and baked apple, damp cardboard or a sherried note has moved to the front. In red wine the rim in the glass browns. If it stings sharp and sour in the nose, of vinegar or nail varnish remover, the microbiological road has been travelled — and then the sink is the only destination left.

Spoiled wine is not dangerous, incidentally; it is merely unpleasant. A bottle that has gone slightly tired is still perfectly good for cooking, and a wine that has oxidised without turning to vinegar does more for a sauce or a braise than it would still do in a glass.

The practical conclusion

If you rarely finish a bottle in an evening, think less about rescue methods and more about what you buy. Half bottles exist for more wines than people assume. And it is worth weighting part of the cellar towards wines that survive two or three days — full whites with acidity, tannic reds, sweet wines in small formats.

One more thing that costs nothing: if a bottle stayed open for a while and still tasted good, that is information about the wine. Write it down and you buy more deliberately next time — in the same way an observation about maturity belongs attached to the wine rather than forgotten (setting up a cellar).
