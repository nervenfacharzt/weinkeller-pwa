Storing wine without a cellar: which spots in a flat actually work

In a flat the question is never where the temperature is perfect, but where it moves least.

Last updated: 2026-07-26

Most of the wine kept in Europe is not kept in cellars. It sits in flats, in cupboards, under beds. That is less damaging than the literature suggests, provided you are honest about what you are asking for. A flat can carry wine comfortably for a few years; what it cannot do is twenty years of maturation. That difference should decide what you buy.

What a flat does to wine

Four things work against wine indoors. First, temperature: 20 to 22 °C (68 to 72 °F) is comfortable for people and fast for wine, and a bottle at room temperature ages noticeably quicker than the same bottle at 12 °C (54 °F). Second, the daily swing: heating on in the morning, off at night, afternoon sun on top. That cycling is what works on the cork, as described in the article on setting up a wine cellar. Third, light, particularly daylight on pale glass. Fourth, dry winter air from central heating, which can fall below 40 per cent relative humidity; corks shrink slowly at that level.

None of this can be solved properly in a flat, but all four improve at once if you choose the right spot — and that is almost always the one furthest from the radiator, the window and the kitchen.

Places that work — and places that do not

Location Typical problem Verdict
Cupboard on an internal or north-facing wall, lowest shelves Very little, as long as no radiator sits beside it The best spot in most flats
Utility room, larder, store cupboard Often too dry, but usually the coolest room in the flat Good
Under the bed, in shallow boxes Dust, and whatever the bedroom temperature happens to be Workable if the room stays unheated
Kitchen cupboards Cooking, waste heat from appliances, large daily swing Only for bottles you will drink within weeks
Windowsill, glazed balcony, conservatory Direct light plus the biggest swing in the building Unsuitable
Above or beside the fridge, oven or washing machine Waste heat and vibration at the same time Unsuitable
Uninsulated loft, garage, garden shed Summer heat and winter frost in the same room Unsuitable

Two details are regularly missed. Warm air sits at the top: the difference between the highest and lowest shelf of a tall cupboard can be several degrees, so wine belongs at the bottom. And an external wall is not automatically better than an internal one — it follows the weather, while the internal wall follows the building, which moves more slowly.

Before rearranging anything, run the test you would run on a cellar: a min/max thermometer in the chosen spot for a fortnight. What matters is not the average but the gap between the readings. Under about three degrees of daily spread is a good result for a flat.

The wine fridge: when it earns its place

A proper wine cabinet actually solves the problem; it is the only purchase in this article that guarantees a steady 12 °C. It starts to make sense where you regularly buy bottles you intend to keep for more than two or three years and the flat offers no spot with a small daily spread. If most of what you buy gets opened within the year, the coolest cupboard will do.

Compressor or thermoelectric

Thermoelectric units (Peltier technology) are quiet and almost vibration-free, but they can only pull the interior a limited amount below the ambient temperature. In a room that reaches 28 °C (82 °F) in summer they will not hold the target, and they run flat out trying. Compressor units cool reliably regardless of the room, but use more electricity and produce some noise and vibration. For anything genuinely intended for storage the compressor is the more robust choice, and an anti-vibration mounting takes the sting out of its one real drawback.

One zone or two

Dual-zone cabinets are serving appliances: one zone at white wine serving temperature, one at red. If the purpose is storage, a single zone held at 12 to 14 °C (54 to 57 °F) is the better use of the space — the split in a dual-zone unit is fixed and usable capacity drops.

What else to check

  • Not a drinks fridge. Appliances that cool to 4 to 8 °C are far too cold for storage and dry the air out.
  • UV-treated glass or a solid door. Only tinted or filtered glass is worth having.
  • Stated capacity is a calculation, not a promise. It assumes slim Bordeaux bottles; with Burgundy or flute bottles in the mix the real figure is considerably lower.
  • Think about where it will stand. Appliances carry a climate class, and a cabinet in an unheated garage runs outside its specification.
  • Sliding shelves rather than fixed ones are the reason you ever find the bottles at the back.

How long you can sensibly store without a cellar

A rule of thumb, not a formula: in a quiet, dark spot at 18 to 20 °C (64 to 68 °F) with a small daily swing, one to three years is unproblematic. After that the wine runs ahead of itself — not spoiled, but further on than the calendar suggests. Five years or more without temperature control is a gamble; a decade needs a cellar or a cabinet.

The wines that suit these conditions are the ones not built for long ageing anyway: whites under screwcap, straightforward reds with moderate tannin, rosé and the everyday stock you buy in order to drink it. What does not suit them is anything that needs years before it becomes enjoyable — classically made Bordeaux, Barolo, serious Riesling, much single-vineyard Blaufränkisch. Parking those in a flat means drinking them halfway through their development.

The most honest answer: do not store at all

For most people without a cellar, the better strategy is not “store it better” but “buy it later”. Mature vintages are available from merchants, from producers holding stock back, and at auction. Someone else has done the storing and you pay for it in the price — not a bad trade when the alternative is storage you cannot control. Instead of twelve bottles that will be good in 2034, buy three that are good now. The cellar shrinks and the hit rate rises.

Taken seriously, that leaves thirty to fifty bottles, and at exactly that size the real flat problem appears: the stock is not in one place but three — some in the cupboard, some under the bed, two cases at your parents'. You know what you own, but no longer quite where it is or how long it has been there. And the bottle you forget goes over the hill faster in a flat than it would in a cellar.
